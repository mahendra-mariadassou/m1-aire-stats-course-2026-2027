Background

In this homework assignment, we will examine the Social Position Index (IPS, for Indice de Positionnement Social, its French name). This is a synthetic index that helps to understand the social status of students based on the professions and social categories (PCS) of their parents. For each PCS, the numerical value of the IPS represents a quantitative summary of a set of socio-economic and cultural attributes related to academic success. The social level of a school is assessed by calculating the average IPS of the students enrolled there.

The social heterogeneity index of a school corresponds to the standard deviation of the IPS of its students. The higher it is, the more diverse the social profile of the students. This index has been calculated since the 2019 school year, only for secondary education institutions.

The data come from the open data platform of Education, Sports, and Youth and were retrieved here in CSV format.

We will start by describing the data using some descriptive statistics before addressing two questions:

  • Is the IPS higher in certain regions?
  • Has the Affelnet reform in Paris had an impact on the IPS of high schools?

Comparing public and private IPS

We’re going to focus on GT high schools in the Academy of Versailles for the last academic year (2021-2022). It’s often claimed that private high schools serve a more privileged audience that public ones. Compare the IPS of public and private high schools to confirm or infirm that claim.

Specify the test and model you use.

This is a t-test of equality of means in two populations. The IPS data are continuous and for lack of a better distribution, we’re going to assume they’re gaussian. There were 54 private high schools and 79 public ones in the Versailles academy in 2021-2002. We thus consider the following model.

  • Note \(X_i\) (\(i = 1,\dots, 79\)) the IPS of the \(i\)-th public highschool.
  • Note \(Y_j\) (\(j = 1,\dots, 54\)) the IPS of the \(j\)-th private highschool.

We assume that the \(X_i\) and the \(Y_j\) are all independant and that \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) and \(Y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\). We want to test \(H_0: \{ \mu_1 \geq \mu_2 \}\) (higher IPS in public high schools) against \(H_1: \{ \mu_1 < \mu_2 \}\) (higher IPS in private high schools). Since we have no information on \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\), we assume that they are unequal to be conservative and use a Welch test (two samples t-test with unequal variances).

Code 
versailles_data <- data |> 
  filter(Academy == "VERSAILLES", Academic_Year == "2021-2022", public_type == "gt")
t.test(IPS ~ Sector, data = versailles_data, alternative = "greater")

The result of the unilateral test conforts the claim that private schools serve a more privileged audience: the p-value is very small (5.781e-10) and we can thus reject the null hypothesis with very high confidence. The estimated means are \(\hat{\mu}_1 = 1232\) for public high schools and \(\hat{\mu}_2 = 1393\) for private ones, so private schools have, on average, an IPS about 160 points higher. The unilateral 95% confidence interval tells us that this difference is, with 95% confidence, larger than \(120\) points — a substantively large gap given that IPS values roughly range between 500 and 1500.

The boxplot in Figure 4 confirms visually what the test establishes statistically: not only is the average IPS higher in private schools, but the two distributions are almost disjoint, with very little overlap between the central 50% of each group.

Change in social heterogeneity

Test whether social heterogeneity increased or decreased after the reform. Specify the test and model you use.

We work on the change in social heterogeneity. It’s a continuous variable and for lack of a better model, we’re going to assume it’s gaussian.

This is a t-test of equality of mean in one population. There were 44 public high schools in the Paris academy. We thus consider the following model.

  • Note \(X_i\) (\(i = 1,\dots, 44\)) the change in social heterogeneity in the \(i\)-th public highschool.

We assume that the \(X_i\) all i.i.d. and that \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with sigma unknown. We want to test \(H_0: \{ \mu \leq 0 \}\) (social heterogeneity decreased between 2021 and 2022) against \(H_1: \{ \mu > 0 \}\) (social heterogeneity increased between 2021 and 2022).

sd_change <- paris_data |>

  pull(change)

The result of the unilateral test is quite significant (p-value = 0.004) and we can thus reject the null hypothesis (decreasing social heterogeneity in each high school) with very high confidence in favor of the alternative (increased social heterogeneity, or more diverse students, in each high school). On average, the within-school standard deviation of IPS increased by \(\hat{\mu} = 0.61\) points, and the one-sided 95% confidence interval places this increase above \(0.24\). The reform seems to have achieved its goal here too.

The histogram in Figure 10 shows that the majority of schools saw their heterogeneity increase or stay roughly constant, while a few schools experienced a marked decrease. This is consistent with the positive mean and the moderate magnitude of the effect.
