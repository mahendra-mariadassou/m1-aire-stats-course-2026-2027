Preamble

Many research directions can be formulated as .blue[questions] with a .alert[yes / no] answer:

  • Is a new variety of tomato more resistant to mildew than the previous one ?
  • Is the expression of gene HER2 large in breast cancer ?
  • Is food cheaper in China than in France ?

You collect .alert[noisy data] about the problem and want to give an evidence based answer to the question. A statistical test (or hypothesis test) is the framework that lets you do so in a principled way — quantifying what it means for the data to support a “yes” rather than a “no”.

This tutorial follows the lecture (§ Tests) and makes its ideas concrete through simulation. It is structured along the four elements of a test (data, model, null hypothesis, decision rule), then walks through a running example (the sex ratio of births in France), before contrasting two ways to decide whether to reject the null: the parametric approach (relying on a known distribution of the test statistic under \(H_0\)) and the permutational / resampling approach (estimating that distribution by shuffling the data).

TipComputing normal and Student quantiles and CDFs

The tests in this tutorial require quantiles and CDFs of the standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (e.g. \(u_{0.975} = 1.960\)) and of the Student distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom (e.g. \(t_{9,0.975} = 2.262\)). In Python (from scipy.stats import norm, t):

  • Normal quantile: norm.ppf(0.975) and CDF: norm.cdf(1.96)
  • Student quantile with \(k\) df: t.ppf(0.975, df=k) and CDF: t.cdf(1.96, df=k)

Remember that the upper \(\alpha\)-quantile \(q_\alpha\) is the value such that \(P(X \le q_\alpha) = \alpha\).

Learning objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • Identify the four elements of a test: the data, the statistical model, the null hypothesis \(H_0\) (with its alternative \(H_1\)), and the decision rule.
  • Explain the two decision errors — Type I (rejecting a true \(H_0\)) and Type II (retaining a false \(H_0\)) — and the necessary trade-off between them.
  • Build a test statistic and calibrate a rejection region from the distribution of the statistic under \(H_0\).
  • Interpret a p-value as the probability, under \(H_0\), of observing a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
  • Run and interpret a two-sample equality-of-means test (Student \(t\)-test, with pooled variance), in both two-sided and one-sided form.
  • Derive the power of a test analytically (noncentral Student), and use it to compare one-sided and two-sided decision rules and to size an experiment.
  • Estimate the null distribution non-parametrically using a permutation test, and compare its p-value with the parametric one.

Section 1 — The Four Elements of a Test

Every statistical test rests on the same four ingredients (lecture § Four elements of a test):

  • Data: \(y_1, \dots, y_n\), a realisation of the random variables \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\);
  • a statistical model: the distribution of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\), depending on some parameters \(\theta\);
  • an assumption: a statement about \(\theta\). This is the null hypothesis \(H_0\) that you usually want to reject, \(H_1\) being the alternative;
  • a decision rule: if \(T = f(Y_1, \dots, Y_n)\) is a test statistic, then \(R\) is a subset of values of \(T\) that is unlikely when \(H_0\) is true. You reject \(H_0\) when \(T\) falls in \(R\).

Let us make these abstract elements concrete on a running example that we will come back to throughout the notebook: the heights of agroengineering students.

The question we will ultimately test is whether the mean height differs between female and male students. Fitting this to the four elements:

  • Data: the heights \((X_1, \dots, X_{n_1})\) of the \(n_1\) female students and \((Y_1, \dots, Y_{n_2})\) of the \(n_2\) male students;
  • Model: \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2)\) and \(Y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)\), with the same variance in both populations, and all heights independent;
  • Null hypothesis: the mean height is the same, \(\mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\);
  • Decision rule: compare the (scaled) mean difference to its distribution under \(H_0\).

The hard work, as the lecture stresses, consists in quantifying what “too far” from \(0\) means — which requires knowing (or estimating) the distribution of the test statistic under \(H_0\).

Section 2 — Decision Errors: Type I and Type II

Because the decision is based on noisy data, it is inherently error-prone. Two mistakes can be made:

  • Type I error (false positive): we reject \(H_0\) when it is in fact true. The probability of this is the significance level \(\alpha\).
  • Type II error (false negative): we retain (fail to reject) \(H_0\) when it is in fact false. Its probability is \(\beta\), and \(1-\beta\) is the power of the test.

There is a necessary trade-off between the two: a more stringent criterion lowers the chance of wrongly rejecting \(H_0\) (fewer Type I errors) but increases the chance of missing a real effect (more Type II errors, less power); a looser criterion does the reverse. The analogy of the lecture is a trial: \(H_0\) is the accused, and you only accumulate evidence against it — you never “accept” the null, you only fail to reject it.

n = 100
R = 5000

The histogram under \(H_0\) shows exactly where the 5% rejection region lives: it is the tail of the null distribution that we declare “too far from \(1/2\)”. When \(H_0\) is true, a point lands there about 5% of the time (the Type I error). When \(H_0\) is false, more of the distribution shifts outside the boundaries, and the fraction that lands there is the power — how often the test correctly says “reject”. Increasing \(n\) (or moving \(p\) further from \(1/2\)) pushes the \(H_1\) distribution further out and raises the power.

Section 3 — Constructing a Test, Step by Step

We now build a complete test following the lecture’s baby example. In France in 2022 there were 351 296 boys and 335 268 girls born (data from INED and INSEE). Is the probability \(p\) of a girl really \(1/2\)? The four elements are:

  • Model: sex of a newborn \(\sim \mathcal{B}(p)\) (a Bernoulli, \(1\) = girl), all children i.i.d.;
  • Null hypothesis: \(H_0: p = 1/2\) (equal chance of boy or girl);
  • Data: \(\hat p = 335268/(335268+351296)\);
  • Decision rule: reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat p\) is too far from \(1/2\);

The construction proceeds in three steps: derive a test statistic, work out its distribution under \(H_0\) (here via the Gaussian approximation to the binomial), and calibrate the region of unlikely values. The hard work consists in quantifying what too far means.

The observed \(\hat p = 0.488\) is far outside the narrow 95% interval \([0.4988, 0.5012]\): under \(H_0\) such a deviation is essentially impossible. So we reject \(H_0\): there is overwhelming evidence that the probability of a girl is not \(1/2\). Note that we have not proven \(p \ne 1/2\) — we have simply shown the data are very unlikely if \(H_0\) were true, which is the correct logical arrow of a significance test.

Section 4 — The p-value

The previous test gives a binary reject / retain verdict. The p-value goes one step further and assesses the significance of the observed statistic: it is the probability, under \(H_0\), of observing a statistic at least as extreme as the one actually observed:

\[ p = P_{H_0}\left( |F - 1/2| > |f - 1/2| \right) . \]

A small p-value means the observed data would be very surprising if \(H_0\) were true — evidence against \(H_0\). A large p-value means the data are quite compatible with \(H_0\).

  • Exercise
  • Solution

  • Objective: compute the p-value for the baby example and for a less extreme example (the youth of Paris), to see the scale of evidence.

  • Instructions:

    • For the babies, use the Gaussian approximation \(F \sim \mathcal{N}(1/2, 1/(4n))\) and compute \[p = P_{H_0}(F < f) + P_{H_0}(F > 1 - f),\] the probability of being at least as far from \(1/2\) as the observed \(f\) (in either direction).
    • Repeat for the Paris youth example: 198 110 boys and 198 905 girls.
    • Compare the two p-values and comment on how extreme each observed deviation is.
Code 
def two_sided_pvalue(n_girls, n_boys):
    n = n_girls + n_boys
    f = n_girls / n
    sd = np.sqrt(1 / (4 * n))
    # P(F < f) + P(F > 2*0.5 - f)  =  P(F < f) + P(F > 1 - f)
    return norm.cdf(f, 0.5, sd) + (1 - norm.cdf(1 - f, 0.5, sd))

f_babies = 335268 / (335268 + 351296)

# Paris youth (0-19)
p_paris = two_sided_pvalue(198905, 198110)
f_paris = 198905 / (198110 + 198905)

print(f"France (babies) : f = {f_babies:.4f}  ->  p-value ~ {p_babies:.2e}")
print(f"Paris (youth)   : f = {f_paris:.4f}  ->  p-value ~ {p_paris:.3f}")

Paris (youth)   : f = 0.5010  ->  p-value ~ 1.793

The two examples illustrate the scale of the p-value. For the babies, \(f = 0.488\) is a huge departure from \(1/2\) given the enormous \(n\) (\(\approx 700\,000\)), so the p-value is astronomically small (\(\approx 5 \times 10^{-89}\)) — not merely unlikely but insanely unlikely under \(H_0\). For the Paris youth, \(f = 0.501\) is a tiny relative deviation, so the p-value is about 21% — a value you would expect to observe roughly once out of every five repetitions even when \(H_0\) is true. A large p-value is not evidence for \(H_0\); it only says the data do not contradict it.

TipFollow-up question

Confirm that with a smaller \(n\) the same deviation is no longer surprising — the p-value grows. This is the practical lesson that statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.

Section 5 — Equality of Means: the Two-Sample \(t\)-Test

We now return to the Agro heights and ask whether the mean height differs between female and male students. Using the four elements:

  • Data: heights \((X_1, \dots, X_{n_1})\) of \(n_1\) female and \((Y_1, \dots, Y_{n_2})\) of \(n_2\) male students;
  • Model: \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2)\) and \(Y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)\) with the same variance \(\sigma^2\), all independent;
  • Null hypothesis: \(\mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\) (same mean height);
  • Decision rule: reject if the (scaled) mean difference \(|T|\) is too far from \(0\).

The crux is the distribution of \(T\) under \(H_0\). The natural statistic is the difference of the sample means, standardized by its standard error. Because the variance is unknown and assumed equal between groups, we use the pooled estimator

\[ S = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1 - 1)S^2_X + (n_2 - 1)S^2_Y}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}}, \]

and under \(H_0\)

\[ T = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{S\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}} \sim \mathcal{T}(n_1 + n_2 - 2), \]

a Student distribution with \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) degrees of freedom. This distribution is what lets us build the rejection region and compute p-values.

    • Compute the two-sided p-value \(2\,(1 - F_{\mathcal{T}}(|t|))\).

Code 
X = Agro.loc[Agro["Gender"] == "F", "Height"].to_numpy()
Y = Agro.loc[Agro["Gender"] == "M", "Height"].to_numpy()
n1, n2 = len(X), len(Y)

xbar, ybar = X.mean(), Y.mean()
S2_X, S2_Y = X.var(ddof=1), Y.var(ddof=1)

S2_pooled = ((n1 - 1) * S2_X + (n2 - 1) * S2_Y) / (n1 + n2 - 2)
S_pooled = np.sqrt(S2_pooled)

t_obs = (xbar - ybar) / (S_pooled * np.sqrt(1 / n1 + 1 / n2))
df = n1 + n2 - 2
t_crit = t.ppf(0.975, df=df)
p_value = 2 * (1 - t.cdf(abs(t_obs), df=df))

print(f"n1 = {n1}, n2 = {n2}          mean F = {xbar:.2f}, mean M = {ybar:.2f}")
print(f"pooled S          : {S_pooled:.3f}")
print(f"t statistic       : {t_obs:.3f}")
print(f"critical |t| (5%) : {t_crit:.3f}   (df = {df})")
print(f"p-value           : {p_value:.3e}")
print(f"Reject H0 (equal means)? -> {abs(t_obs) > t_crit}")

# Cross-check with scipy
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind
res = ttest_ind(X, Y, equal_var=True)

Here the two groups differ by about \(8\) cm (the means we built into the simulation), which is huge relative to the standard error of a few fractions of a centimetre. Unsurprisingly, \(|t|\) is enormous, the p-value is far below \(0.05\), and we reject equal means. The cross-check with scipy.stats.ttest_ind(..., equal_var=True) matches the by-hand computation exactly — the pooled-variance Student test is scipy’s default when equal_var=True. What you should retain is why it works: knowing the distribution of \(T\) under \(H_0\) turns “how far is too far?” into an arithmetic question about the Student quantile.

Section 6 — One-Sided Test for the Comparison of Means

In Section 5 the alternative was two-sided: we rejected equal means for any large deviation, whether \(\bar X_F < \bar X_M\) or the reverse. Here we consider the natural one-sided question prompted by the data: are men, on average, taller than women? In symbols

\[ H_0: \mu_M - \mu_F \le 0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \mu_M - \mu_F > 0 . \]

The statistic \(T\) is the same as in Section 5, but the direction of the evidence changes both the decision rule and the p-value:

  • Decision rule: reject \(H_0\) only when \(T\) is large and positive, i.e. \(T > t_{n_1+n_2-2,\, 0.95}\) — the upper \(5\%\) quantile, not the upper \(2.5\%\) one used for the two-sided test;
  • p-value: now a one-sided tail probability \[ p = P_{H_0}(T > t_{\text{obs}}) = 1 - F_{\mathcal{T}}(t_{\text{obs}}), \] rather than the sum of the two tails.

Intuition: if we have committed to the question “are men taller?”, only a large positive \(T\) is evidence against \(H_0\). A large negative \(T\) (men shorter) is completely irrelevant to that question, so it contributes nothing to the p-value and lies inside the rejection region’s complement — such a result can never lead to rejecting this particular \(H_0\).

Because the evidence against this \(H_0\) lives in a single tail, the one-sided p-value is (to within a factor of 2) half the two-sided one, and the rejection threshold \(t_{0.95}\) is smaller than \(t_{0.975}\). With the Agro data both are astronomically small, but the essential point is general: committing to a direction before seeing the data makes the test more powerful (it needs a smaller deviation to reject), at the price of being unable to detect an effect in the opposite direction. The direction must therefore be chosen from the research question in advance, not from the data — choosing the side that suits an observed result inflates the Type I error and is a form of p-hacking. Narrowing the alternative to one side is legitimate only when the opposite direction is genuinely uninteresting or impossible.

TipFollow-up question
  • Reverse the question and test \(H_0: \mu_F - \mu_M \le 0\) (women taller). Now the critical value is \(-t_{0.95}\) and you reject for \(T < -t_{0.95}\). Because the observed \(T\) here is hugely positive, this one-sided test can never reject — its p-value is essentially 1. This makes the trade-off concrete: a one-sided test in the wrong direction is completely blind to the effect, which is why choosing the direction from the data is so dangerous.

Section 7 — The Power of a Test

Section 2 introduced power as \(1-\beta\): the probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when it is in fact false — i.e. the test correctly detecting the effect it is designed to find. A test with good power is what lets you trust a “failure to reject” as informative (the effect really is small/absent) rather than as mere lack of evidence. Here we make the concept precise and analytic — no simulation — and use it to compare one-sided and two-sided decision rules.

For a fixed test and significance level \(\alpha\), the power depends on four things:

  • Effect size — the true distance between the parameter and \(H_0\), i.e. the true value of \(\delta = \mu_M - \mu_F\);
  • Sample size \(n_1, n_2\) — more data shrinks the standard error, concentrating the statistic around its true value;
  • Significance level \(\alpha\) — a stricter level moves the rejection boundary out, lowering power;
  • Direction of the alternative — one-sided vs two-sided, which changes where the rejection region lies.

Deriving power analytically. Under \(H_0\) the statistic satisfies \(T \sim \mathcal{T}(n_1 + n_2 - 2)\). When \(H_0\) is false, with a true difference \(\delta = \mu_M - \mu_F\), the statistic is no longer centred at \(0\): it follows a noncentral Student distribution \[ T \sim \mathcal{T}_{n_1+n_2-2}(\lambda) \qquad \text{with noncentrality} \quad \lambda = \frac{\delta}{\sigma\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}}, \] where \(\sigma\) is the common within-group standard deviation. The power is simply the probability that this shifted statistic lands in the rejection region:

  • two-sided (\(H_1: \delta \ne 0\)), rejecting when \(|T| > t_{n_1+n_2-2,\, 0.975}\): \[ \text{power} = P\big( T < -t_{.975} \big) + P\big( T > t_{.975} \big), \] evaluated under \(\mathcal{T}_{df}(\lambda)\);
  • one-sided (\(H_1: \delta > 0\)), rejecting when \(T > t_{n_1+n_2-2,\, 0.95}\): \[ \text{power} = P\big( T > t_{.95} \big) \] under the same noncentral Student.

With the noncentral \(t\) distribution available (e.g. scipy.stats.nct), both powers can be computed exactly for any effect size.

The two curves make the trade-offs of Section 6 quantitative. For any \(\delta > 0\), the one-sided test has higher power than the two-sided one at the same \(\alpha\): it concentrates its entire 5% rejection mass on one side, so it needs a smaller true difference to reach any given power (and correspondingly reaches 80% power at a smaller \(\delta\) in the plot above). The price is blindness in the opposite direction, as the “wrong-direction” follow-up in Section 6 showed. Power also grows with \(n\) and \(\delta\), and shrinks as \(\alpha\) or \(\sigma\) grow — check this by re-running with a different sigma or n. Note also that at \(\delta = 0\) (no effect) both “powers” collapse to the Type I error rate \(\alpha = 0.05\): this is the sanity check that the derivation integrates correctly.

Section 8 — Permutational p-value

The \(t\)-test above relies on knowing the distribution of \(T\) (or of \(\bar X - \bar Y\)) under \(H_0\). But what if we don’t know that distribution? The lecture’s answer: estimate it using simulations and resampling — a permutation test.

The idea is simple. Under \(H_0\) (no association between height and gender), the gender labels carry no information about height. So we can sample the null distribution by repeatedly shuffling the gender labels, recomputing the statistic \(\Delta = \bar X - \bar Y\) on each shuffled dataset, and collecting the resulting values \(\Delta_{(1)}, \dots, \Delta_{(N)}\). These are the differences we would expect just by chance under \(H_0\). We then compare the actually observed \(\Delta\) to this distribution and compute the p-value as

\[ p = \frac{1 + \#\{i \text{ s.t. } |\Delta_{(i)}| > |\Delta|\}}{N + 1}. \]

Permutational p-value     : 0.0001   (p = 1/10000 if never exceeded)

The permutational p-value estimates the same quantity as the parametric \(t\)-test, but without assuming a Gaussian model or a Student distribution: it only uses the data and the shuffling to construct the null. Here the two approaches agree (both tiny), because the Gaussian model is appropriate. Where they differ is when the parametric assumption is suspect or when the statistic is awkward to study analytically (medians, correlations, etc.) — the permutation test always works, at the cost of being approximate/conditional on the observed data.

Summary and Key Takeaways

This tutorial illustrated the principle of hypothesis testing through simulation and worked examples. The key points:

  1. A test has four elements. Data, a statistical model, a null hypothesis \(H_0\) stated in terms of model parameters, and a decision rule built from a test statistic. The whole craft lies in quantifying what values of the statistic are too far from \(H_0\).

  2. Decisions are error-prone. A Type I error rejects a true \(H_0\) (probability \(\alpha\)); a Type II error retains a false \(H_0\) (probability \(\beta\), with power \(1-\beta\)). There is a necessary trade-off between them, controlled by the significance level, the sample size, and the effect size. You never accept the null — you only fail to reject it.

  3. The rejection region comes from the null distribution. Once you know (or estimate) the distribution of the test statistic under \(H_0\), the rejection region is a tail of that distribution. Whether that distribution is derived analytically (Gaussian approximation, Student \(t\)) or estimated by simulation (permutation), the logic is identical.

  4. The p-value measures the extremity of the data under \(H_0\). It is the probability, under \(H_0\), of observing a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. A very small p-value is evidence against \(H_0\); a large one only says the data do not contradict it. And with huge \(n\), any tiny effect becomes “significant” — statistical significance is not practical importance. For a one-sided alternative the p-value is a single tail and (to within a factor of 2) half the two-sided one; committing to a direction before seeing the data is more powerful but must be decided a priori, never from the data.

  5. Power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false \(H_0\). It can be computed analytically from the noncentral Student distribution of the statistic under \(H_1\), as a function of the effect size, sample size, level \(\alpha\), and direction of the alternative. The one-sided test is more powerful than the two-sided one for any effect in its declared direction (it needs a smaller \(\delta\) to reach a given power), at the price of blindness in the opposite direction. The same formulas drive sample-size planning for experiments.

  6. Two roads to the same destination. The two-sample \(t\)-test calibrates the scaled mean difference against a Student distribution (a parametric assumption); the permutation test calibrates the same difference against its empirical distribution obtained by shuffling labels. When the parametric model holds they agree; the permutation test is the safer default when it does not.

The reusable takeaway is the logic of a test: frame the question as a hypothesis about a parameter, build a statistic that is sensitive to it, calibrate its distribution under \(H_0\), and report how extreme the observed data are. If you haven’t already, work through the follow-up questions — the power-versus-\(n\) simulation and the permutation/parametric comparison are the ones that most directly test whether the reasoning has landed.
