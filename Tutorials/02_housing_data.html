Preamble

The objective of this notebook is to explore the concepts of sampling, resampling, and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) through Python-based numerical examples based on a toy dataset. We will also extend our analysis to multivariate relationships and advanced sampling techniques.

Learning objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • Describe the structure of a multivariate dataset and check the quality of its variables.
  • Explain the difference between the population distribution and a sample distribution.
  • Explain and illustrate the Central Limit Theorem numerically, and the Law of Large Numbers.
  • Compare simple random, stratified and systematic sampling methods.
  • Use bootstrap resampling to estimate sampling distributions and build confidence intervals, and reason about the bias-variance tradeoff.
  • Use a permutation test to perform a hypothesis test.

From the Population to a Sample

So far we have worked on the whole dataset, which we can think of as our population (the 506 Boston census tracts). In practice we rarely have access to the whole population — we observe only a sample and must infer population quantities from it. This is the core of statistical inference.

We begin with the simplest tool: simple random sampling (drawing observations without replacement, each equally likely).

Code 
def bootstrap_sample_mean(data, n_iterations=1000, sample_size=100):
    means = []
    for _ in range(n_iterations):
        sample = data.sample(sample_size, replace=True)
        means.append(sample.mean())
    return means

# Bootstrap distribution of the mean
bootstrap_means = bootstrap_sample_mean(data['medv'], n_iterations=1000, sample_size=100)

Illustrating the Central Limit Theorem

What’s the CLT ?

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) says that if you take a bunch of random samples from any population (no matter how it’s shaped—whether it’s skewed, uniform, or weird-looking) and calculate the average (or mean) of those samples, the distribution of those sample means will (under very mild conditions) eventually look like a normal distribution (bell-shaped curve) as long as your sample size is big enough.

In other words:

  • If you keep sampling and averaging, the averages will cluster around the true population mean.

  • This works no matter what the original data looks like, as long as the sample size is reasonably large.

So, the CLT helps us use averages from samples to make predictions about the whole population, which is super useful in statistics!

def sample_means_by_size(data, n_iterations=1000, sizes=[30, 50, 100, 500, 1000]):
    means_dict = {}
    for size in sizes:
        means = []
        for _ in range(n_iterations):
            sample = data.sample(size, replace=True)
            means.append(sample.mean())
        means_dict[size] = means
    return means_dict

Bias-Variance Tradeoff in Resampling

  • Exercise
  • Solution

  • Objective: Introduce the bias-variance tradeoff and its relevance to resampling techniques.

  • Instructions:

    • Take bootstrap samples from the dataset and compute the sample mean and variance.

    • Increase the number of bootstrap iterations and observe how the bias and variance of the estimate change.

    • Explore the tradeoff between high variance (due to small samples) and high bias (due to poor estimation methods).

Code 
def bootstrap_bias_variance(data, sample_size=100, n_iterations=1000):
    sample_means = []
    for _ in range(n_iterations):
        sample = data.sample(sample_size, replace=True)
        sample_means.append(sample.mean())
    bias = np.mean(sample_means) - data.mean()
    variance = np.var(sample_means)
    return bias, variance

# Calculate bias and variance for different bootstrap iterations
iterations_list = [100, 500, 1000, 5000]
bias_values = []
variance_values = []

for n_iter in iterations_list:
    bias, var = bootstrap_bias_variance(data['medv'], sample_size=100, n_iterations=n_iter)
    bias_values.append(bias)
    variance_values.append(var)
    print(f"Iterations: {n_iter}, Bias: {bias:.4f}, Variance: {var:.4f}")

# Plot bias and variance
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))

Hypothesis Testing using Resampling

  • Exercise
  • Solution

  • Objective: Use resampling methods (e.g., permutation testing) to perform hypothesis testing.

  • Instructions:

    • Formulate a hypothesis about the data (e.g., the mean house price in chas = 1 is different from chas = 0).

    • Perform a permutation test by randomly shuffling the chas labels and computing the mean difference.

    • Compare the observed mean difference to the permutation distribution to determine if the difference is statistically significant. You can do that using the p-value, the probability that a sample from the permutation distribution exceeds (in absolute value) the observed mean.

Code 
def permutation_test(data, group_col, value_col, n_permutations=1000):
    # Observed difference in means
    observed_diff = data.groupby(group_col)[value_col].mean().diff().iloc[-1]

    perm_diffs = []
    for _ in range(n_permutations):
        permuted = data.copy()
        permuted[group_col] = np.random.permutation(permuted[group_col])
        perm_diff = permuted.groupby(group_col)[value_col].mean().diff().iloc[-1]
        perm_diffs.append(perm_diff)

    # Plot permutation distribution
    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
    sns.histplot(perm_diffs, kde=True, bins=30, alpha=0.7, color='lightgreen')
    plt.axvline(observed_diff, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, label=f'Observed Diff: {observed_diff:.2f}')
    plt.axvline(-observed_diff, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, label=f'-Observed Diff: {-observed_diff:.2f}')
    plt.title(f"Permutation Test Distribution\n(observed diff = {observed_diff:.2f})", fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
    plt.xlabel("Difference in Means", fontsize=12)
    plt.ylabel("Density", fontsize=12)
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    plt.show()

    # Calculate p-value
    p_value = np.mean(np.abs(perm_diffs) >= np.abs(observed_diff))
    return observed_diff, p_value

# Perform permutation test on 'chas' vs 'medv'
obs_diff, p_val = permutation_test(data, 'chas', 'medv')
print(f"Observed Difference: {obs_diff:.4f}")
print(f"P-value: {p_val:.4f}")

if p_val < 0.05:
    print("The difference is statistically significant (p < 0.05)")
else:
    print("The difference is not statistically significant (p >= 0.05)")

Demonstrating the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

  • Exercise
  • Solution

  • Objective: Illustrate how the Law of Large Numbers works by showing that sample means converge to the population mean as sample size increases.

  • Instructions:

    • Take repeated samples of increasing sizes and plot how the sample mean converges to the population mean.
Code 
def law_of_large_numbers(data, max_sample_size=500, step=50):
    sample_sizes = range(50, max_sample_size, step)
    sample_means = []

    for size in sample_sizes:
        sample = data.sample(size, replace=False)
        sample_means.append(sample.mean())

    # Plot sample means vs. sample size
    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
    plt.plot(sample_sizes, sample_means, marker='o', linewidth=2, markersize=6, color='blue')
    plt.axhline(data.mean(), color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, label=f'Population Mean: {data.mean():.2f}')
    plt.title("Law of Large Numbers: Sample Means Converging to Population Mean", fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')
    plt.xlabel("Sample Size", fontsize=12)
    plt.ylabel("Sample Mean", fontsize=12)
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    plt.show()

Summary and Key Takeaways

This tutorial has built up, step by step, the core toolkit of statistical inference. Each section rests on the previous one:

  1. Data Exploration: we first checked data quality and visualized medv on its own, then examined how it relates to the other variables. Knowing your data is a prerequisite to any inference.

  2. From population to sample: we distinguished the full dataset (our population) from the samples we draw from it, and saw that larger samples approximate the population distribution better.

  3. Central Limit Theorem: the distribution of sample means becomes Normal as the sample size grows, no matter the population shape — the theoretical engine behind inference on means.

  4. The Law of Large Numbers: sample means converge to the population mean as the sample size increases, and the standard error shrinks like \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

  5. Bootstrap resampling: by resampling with replacement from the observed sample, we can approximate the sampling distribution of any statistic (e.g. the mean) and build confidence intervals.

  6. Comparing sampling methods: simple random, stratified and systematic sampling differ in precision; their choice matters for real surveys and experiments.

  7. Hypothesis testing by permutation: we used resampling to test whether a difference between groups (e.g. by chas) could be due to chance alone, via a p-value.

Taken together, these ideas — sampling distributions, the CLT, the bootstrap, the LLN, and permutation tests — form the foundation of modern statistical inference. They are the building blocks you will reuse throughout the course, including when we move on to predictive modelling.
