TipComputing normal quantiles and CDFs

Both exercises require the quantiles of the standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (e.g. \(u_{0.95} = 1.645\), \(u_{0.975} = 1.960\)) and its cumulative distribution function. The upper \(\alpha\)-quantile \(u_\alpha\) (the value such that \(P(Z \le u_\alpha) = \alpha\) for \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\)) is obtained as follows, and you can also evaluate the CDF at any point.

In R

  • Normal quantile: qnorm(0.975) and CDF: pnorm(1.897)
  • Power of a test: 1 - pnorm(c, mean = p1, sd = sqrt(p1*(1-p1)/n))

In Python (from scipy.stats import norm)

  • Normal quantile: norm.ppf(0.975) and CDF: norm.cdf(1.897)
  • Power of a test: 1 - norm.cdf(c, loc = p1, scale = sqrt(p1*(1-p1)/n))

You can check that qnorm(0.975) = norm.ppf(0.975) = \(1.960\) and that qnorm(0.95) = norm.ppf(0.95) = \(1.645\); the CDF at the observed \(z\) of Exercise 1 gives the p-values of that exercise.

Exercise 1: One-Sided and Two-Sided Tests

A survey company polls a sample of \(n = 1000\) voters and asks them a simple yes/no question. In the sample, \(530\) voters answer “yes”, so the observed proportion is \(\hat{p} = 530/1000 = 0.53\). We model the answers as

\[X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \mathcal{B}(p) \quad \text{i.i.d.},\]

so that \(E[X_i] = p\), \(V[X_i] = p(1-p)\), and \(\widehat{P} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i\) is the sample proportion. We want to test whether the population proportion \(p\) differs from \(1/2\). Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: p = 1/2\), the CLT gives the approximate distribution of the test statistic

\[Z = \frac{\widehat{P} - 1/2}{\sqrt{1/(4n)}} \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1),\]

because under \(H_0\) the variance of \(\widehat{P}\) is exactly \(V[\widehat{P}] = p_0(1-p_0)/n = (1/2)(1/2)/n = 1/(4n)\), with no plug-in needed. With \(n = 1000\) the standard deviation of \(\widehat{P}\) under \(H_0\) is \(\sqrt{1/(4n)} \approx 0.01581\).

  1. Two-sided test. Set up the test of \(H_0: p = 1/2\) against \(H_1: p \ne 1/2\) at level \(\alpha = 0.05\). Recall the rejection region in terms of the statistic \(Z\) and the critical value \(u_{0.975}\); apply it to the observed value \(z\) and conclude.
  2. One-sided test. Set up the test of \(H_0: p = 1/2\) against \(H_1: p > 1/2\) at level \(\alpha = 0.05\). Give the (different) rejection region and the critical value \(u_{0.95}\); apply it to the same \(z\) and conclude.
  3. p-values. Compute the p-values of the one-sided test (\(H_1: p > 1/2\)) and of the two-sided test, and compare them. In particular, verify the approximate relation \(p_{\text{two}} \approx 2\, p_{\text{one}}\).
  4. Discussion. The data are identical in questions 1 and 2, yet the two tests do not agree. Explain in words why the one-sided test is “easier” here: where does the level \(\alpha\) go in each case, and what becomes of the critical value and of the p-value?
  5. (Advanced) Wrong direction. Repeat the one-sided calculation for the other one-sided problem \(H_1: p < 1/2\). Compute the corresponding p-value and conclude. What does this illustrate about the blindness of a one-sided test in the wrong direction, and about the danger of choosing the direction after looking at the data (p-hacking)?
  6. (Advanced) General relation. Show that for a normal statistic the two-sided p-value is \(p_{\text{two}} = 2\min(p_{\text{one}}, 1 - p_{\text{one}})\), where \(p_{\text{one}}\) is the p-value of the “right” one-sided test. Deduce the observed counts \(x\) (number of “yes” answers out of \(n = 1000\)) for which the two-sided test rejects while the one-sided right test (\(H_1: p > 1/2\)) does not, i.e. the counts such that \(|z| > 1.96\) while \(z < 1.645\). Which side of the boundary separates the two tests?

Solution 1

Work through these steps after attempting the exercise on your own. Each step corresponds to an instruction in Section 1.

Observed statistic

The observed proportion is \(\hat{p} = 0.53\) and the standard deviation under \(H_0\) is \(\sqrt{1/(4n)} = \sqrt{0.00025} \approx 0.01581\), hence the observed value of the test statistic is

\[z = \frac{\hat{p} - 1/2}{\sqrt{1/(4n)}} = \frac{0.03}{0.01581} \approx 1.897.\]

Two-sided test

We test \(H_0: p = 1/2\) against \(H_1: p \ne 1/2\) at level \(\alpha = 0.05\). Rejecting when the standardized distance from \(1/2\) is large, the rejection region is

\[\mathcal{R} = \left\{ |Z| > u_{0.975} \right\}, \qquad u_{0.975} = 1.960.\]

The observed value \(|z| = 1.897\) is smaller than \(1.960\), so we do not reject \(H_0\) at level 5%: the data are compatible with a population proportion of \(1/2\).

One-sided test

We test \(H_0: p = 1/2\) against \(H_1: p > 1/2\). Now only large positive deviations count as evidence against \(H_0\), so all the level \(\alpha\) is spent on the right tail and the critical value is the smaller quantile \(u_{0.95}\):

\[\mathcal{R} = \left\{ Z > u_{0.95} \right\}, \qquad u_{0.95} = 1.645.\]

The observed value \(z = 1.897\) exceeds \(1.645\), so we reject \(H_0\) at level 5% and conclude that the proportion of “yes” answers is significantly larger than \(1/2\).

p-values

With \(Z \approx \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) under \(H_0\):

\[p_{\text{one}} = P(Z \ge z) = 1 - \Phi(z) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.897) \approx 0.029,\]

\[p_{\text{two}} = 2\,P(Z \ge |z|) = 2\,(1 - \Phi(|z|)) \approx 2 \times 0.029 \approx 0.058.\]

As announced \(p_{\text{two}} \approx 2\, p_{\text{one}}\): the two-sided test must count both tails, so its p-value is (approximately) twice the one-sided one. The verdicts match the rejection regions: \(p_{\text{one}} = 0.029 < 0.05\) rejects, \(p_{\text{two}} = 0.058 > 0.05\) retains.

Discussion

The data are identical but the tests answer different questions, and this changes where the level \(\alpha = 0.05\) is spent:

  • the two-sided test splits the level between the two tails (\(\alpha/2 = 0.025\) on each side), so its critical value is \(u_{0.975} = 1.96\);
  • the one-sided test spends all of \(\alpha\) on the single tail that matches the direction of \(H_1\), so its critical value is smaller, \(u_{0.95} = 1.645\).

Equivalently, for the same data the one-sided p-value is half the two-sided one. The observed \(z \approx 1.897\) falls between the two critical values, so it is significant for the one-sided question but not for the two-sided one. This is the pedagogical core of the exercise: at the boundary of significance, the direction of the alternative decides the verdict. The direction must therefore be fixed before looking at the data.

Wrong direction (Advanced)

For the problem \(H_1: p < 1/2\), the rejection region is \(\{Z < -\,u_{0.95}\}\) and the p-value counts only the left tail:

\[p_{\text{wrong}} = P(Z \le z) = \Phi(z) \approx \Phi(1.897) \approx 0.97.\]

The p-value is \(\approx 0.97\), essentially 1: one can never reject with this test, no matter how far above \(1/2\) the observed proportion lies. This is the mirror image of the blindness described in the lectures: a one-sided test is completely blind to evidence pointing in the opposite direction of \(H_1\). Since \(\Phi(z) \to 1\) when \(z \to +\infty\), the p-value even increases as the evidence against \(H_0\) (in the other direction) grows stronger. Choosing the direction after seeing the data — and then reporting the smaller of the two one-sided p-values — is a form of p-hacking: it inflates the type I error rate far beyond the nominal \(\alpha\), because under \(H_0\) the minimum of the two one-sided p-values is \(\mathcal{U}(0, 1/2)\) by symmetry (below the usual uniform).

General relation and borderline counts (Advanced)

For a normal statistic with observed value \(z\), call \(p_{\text{one}} = 1 - \Phi(z)\) the p-value of the right-tail test \(H_1: p > 1/2\). If \(z \ge 0\) then the left-tail p-value is \(1 - p_{\text{one}}\) and the two-sided p-value is \(2(1 - \Phi(z)) = 2 p_{\text{one}} = 2 \min(p_{\text{one}}, 1 - p_{\text{one}})\). If instead \(z < 0\), the roles are exchanged: \(p_{\text{one}} = 1 - \Phi(z) > 1/2\), the relevant tail is the left one with p-value \(\Phi(z) = 1 - p_{\text{one}}\), and \(p_{\text{two}} = 2(1 - p_{\text{one}})\). Hence in all cases

\[p_{\text{two}} = 2 \min(p_{\text{one}},\ 1 - p_{\text{one}}).\]

For the borderline counts, the two-sided test rejects iff \(|z| > 1.96\), i.e. \(\hat{p} = x/1000 < 0.4690\) or \(x/1000 > 0.5310\); the right one-sided test rejects iff \(x/1000 > 0.5260\). The counts where the two-sided test rejects while the right one-sided test retains are therefore the low ones, \(x/1000 \le 0.4690\), i.e. \(x \le 469\) (on the high side, \(x \ge 531\) both tests reject). Check the boundary:

\[x = 469:\quad z = \frac{0.469 - 0.5}{0.01581} \approx -1.961,\qquad |z| = 1.961 > 1.96,\quad z < 1.645,\]

so the two-sided test rejects and the (right) one-sided test does not; for \(x = 470\), \(|z| \approx 1.897 < 1.96\) and both tests retain. Symmetrically, for \(531 \le x \le 1000\) the two-sided test rejects and the right one-sided test rejects — only the low announcements \(x \le 469\) separate the two tests.

Exercise 2: Power of a Proportion Test

We reuse the same setting as Exercise 1: \(n = 1000\) independent Bernoulli answers with proportion of “yes” \(p\), and we test \(H_0: p = 1/2\) at level \(\alpha = 0.05\) using the CLT approximation

\[Z = \frac{\widehat{P} - 1/2}{\sqrt{1/(4n)}} \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{under } H_0.\]

The type II error \(\beta\) is the probability of not rejecting \(H_0\) when it is false, and the power of the test is \(1 - \beta = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_1)\). Under the alternative with true proportion \(p_1 \ne 1/2\), the CLT gives \(\widehat{P} \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(p_1,\ p_1(1-p_1)/n)\), so the power can be computed with the normal CDF. Throughout, \(u_{0.975} = 1.960\) and \(u_{0.95} = 1.645\).

  1. Rejection regions on the \(\hat{p}\) scale. Translate the critical values of Exercise 1 back to the scale of the estimated proportion: give the threshold \(c\) such that the one-sided test rejects when \(\widehat{P} > c\), and the interval \([c_1; c_2]\) such that the two-sided test rejects when \(\widehat{P} \notin [c_1; c_2]\), for \(\alpha = 0.05\).
  2. Power at \(p_1 = 0.53\). Assume the true proportion is \(p_1 = 0.53\). Compute the power of the one-sided and of the two-sided tests, using 1 - norm.cdf(c, loc = p1, scale = sqrt(p1*(1-p1)/n)) (and the symmetric two-tail formula). Compare the two powers and interpret.
  3. Power curve. Write the commands that evaluate the power of both tests over a grid of values of \(p_1\) between, say, 0.40 and 0.60, to produce a power curve. Describe what the curve looks like: behaviour as \(p_1 \to 1/2\), monotonicity, and effect of \(n\).
  4. (Advanced) Sample-size planning. To design a survey with power \(1 - \beta = 0.80\) at \(p_1 = 0.53\), derive from the normal approximation the required sample size

\[n \approx \frac{\left( u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)} + u_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)} \right)^2}{(p_1 - p_0)^2}\]

for the one-sided test (and the analogous formula with \(u_{1-\alpha/2}\) for the two-sided test), then compute \(n\) in both cases. Which test needs fewer observations, and why?

  1. (Advanced) Effect of \(\alpha\). Recompute the power at \(p_1 = 0.53\) when the level is \(\alpha = 0.01\) instead of \(0.05\), for both tests. What happens, and what does this reveal about the trade-off between type I and type II errors?

Solution 2

Work through these steps after attempting the exercise on your own. Each step corresponds to an instruction in Section 3.

Rejection regions on the \(\hat{p}\) scale

Under \(H_0\), \(\widehat{P} \approx \mathcal{N}(1/2,\ 1/(4n))\) with \(\sqrt{1/(4n)} \approx 0.01581\), so

  • one-sided test (\(H_1: p > 1/2\)): rejects when

\[c = \frac{1}{2} + u_{0.95}\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}} = 0.5 + 1.645 \times 0.01581 \approx 0.5260,\]

i.e. when \(\widehat{P} > 0.5260\);

  • two-sided test: rejects when \(\widehat{P}\) falls outside

\[\left[ \frac{1}{2} - u_{0.975}\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}};\ \frac{1}{2} + u_{0.975}\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}} \right] \approx [\,0.4690;\ 0.5310\,].\]

Power at \(p_1 = 0.53\)

Under \(H_1\) with \(p_1 = 0.53\),

\[\widehat{P} \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\left(0.53,\ \frac{0.53\times 0.47}{1000}\right), \qquad \sqrt{\frac{0.53\times 0.47}{1000}} \approx 0.01578.\]

One-sided. The test rejects when \(\widehat{P} > c = 0.5260\), hence

\[1 - \beta_{\text{one}} = P(\widehat{P} > 0.5260) = 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{0.5260 - 0.53}{0.01578}\right) = 1 - \Phi(-0.253) \approx 0.60.\]

Two-sided. The test rejects when \(\widehat{P} \notin [0.4690;\ 0.5310]\), hence

\[1 - \beta_{\text{two}} = 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{0.5310 - 0.53}{0.01578}\right) + \Phi\left(\frac{0.4690 - 0.53}{0.01578}\right) \approx 1 - \Phi(0.063) + \Phi(-3.867) \approx 0.475.\]

The one-sided test (power \(\approx 0.60\)) is noticeably more powerful than the two-sided test (power \(\approx 0.47\)) at the same alternative \(p_1 = 0.53\) in its declared direction. This is the quantitative counterpart of Exercise 1: the one-sided test spends all of \(\alpha\) on the interesting side, lowering the threshold from \(0.5310\) to \(0.5260\); the two-sided test must spend half of its level on the tail that contradicts \(H_1\) and is therefore almost never used. The price of one-sidedness is blindness in the opposite direction: the same test has power \(\to 0\) when \(p_1 < 1/2\).

Power curve

The commands below evaluate the power of the two tests over a grid of true proportions (Python syntax, norm imported as in the callout above):

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

n = 1000
p1_grid = np.linspace(0.40, 0.60, 201)
sd0 = np.sqrt(1/(4*n))
sd1 = np.sqrt(p1_grid*(1-p1_grid)/n)

# one-sided: reject when Phat > c
pow_one = 1 - norm.cdf(0.5 + norm.ppf(0.95)*sd0, loc = p1_grid, scale = sd1)
# two-sided: reject outside [c1, c2]
pow_two = (1 - norm.cdf(0.5 + norm.ppf(0.975)*sd0, loc = p1_grid, scale = sd1)
           + norm.cdf(0.5 - norm.ppf(0.975)*sd0, loc = p1_grid, scale = sd1))

The curve has the following qualitative features:

  • as \(p_1 \to p_0 = 1/2\) the power tends to the level \(\alpha = 0.05\) (when there is nothing to detect, the rejection probability is just the type I error rate);
  • the power increases monotonically as \(|p_1 - p_0|\) grows, approaching 1 for far-away alternatives;
  • for a fixed alternative, the power increases with \(n\): larger samples shrink the standard deviation \(\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)/n}\), pushing the alternative distribution further into the rejection region;
  • the one-sided curve lies above the two-sided one on the right half (its declared direction) and, symmetrically, well below it on the left half — the two-sided test is more powerful for alternatives in the opposite direction.

Sample-size planning (Advanced)

Write \(p_0 = 1/2\) and let \(u_{1-\alpha}\), \(u_{1-\beta}\) denote the normal quantiles of the levels \(\alpha\) (of the test) and \(\beta\) (of the power). Repeating the reasoning of Exercise 2 with a generic sample size \(n\), the one-sided test rejects when \(\widehat{P} > p_0 + u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\), and the power at \(p_1\) is

\[1 - \beta = P\left( \widehat{P} > p_0 + u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}} \right) = 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{p_0 - p_1 + u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}{\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)/n}} \right).\]

Setting this equal to \(1 - \beta\) gives \(\Phi^{-1}(1-\beta) = u_{1-\beta}\) and

\[p_1 - p_0 = u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}} + u_{1-\beta}\sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n}},\]

hence, solving for \(n\),

\[n \approx \frac{\left( u_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)} + u_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)} \right)^2}{(p_1 - p_0)^2}.\]

The two-sided test uses \(u_{1-\alpha/2}\) instead of \(u_{1-\alpha}\) in the numerator (and the formula is then approximate, valid when the far-tail term is negligible). With \(p_0 = 1/2\), \(p_1 = 0.53\), \(1 - \beta = 0.80\) (so \(\beta = 0.20\) and \(u_{0.80} = 0.842\)), \(\alpha = 0.05\) (\(u_{0.95} = 1.645\), \(u_{0.975} = 1.960\)), and \(\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)} = \sqrt{0.2491} \approx 0.499\):

  • one-sided: \(n \approx \left( 1.645 \times 0.5 + 0.842 \times 0.499 \right)^2 / 0.03^2 \approx (0.822 + 0.420)^2 / 0.0009 \approx (1.242)^2/0.0009 \approx 1714\);
  • two-sided: \(n \approx \left( 1.960 \times 0.5 + 0.842 \times 0.499 \right)^2 / 0.03^2 \approx (0.980 + 0.420)^2 / 0.0009 \approx (1.400)^2/0.0009 \approx 2178\).

The one-sided test needs about \(n \approx 1700\) observations versus about \(n \approx 2200\) for the two-sided test: for the same power, the test that is allowed to look in both directions must be larger. Conversely, at the same sample size the one-sided test reaches a given power the two-sided test can only match with a larger sample. (The exact normal computation gives \(1715\) and \(2178\) respectively.)

Effect of the level \(\alpha\) (Advanced)

Repeating the power computation with \(\alpha = 0.01\) (so \(u_{0.99} = 2.326\) and \(u_{0.995} = 2.576\)) at \(p_1 = 0.53\):

  • the one-sided threshold moves from \(c = 0.5260\) to \(c' = 0.5 + 2.326 \times 0.01581 \approx 0.5368\), and the power drops from \(\approx 0.60\) to \(1 - \Phi\left(\frac{0.5368 - 0.53}{0.01578}\right) = 1 - \Phi(0.430) \approx 0.33\);
  • the two-sided thresholds move to \([\,0.4593;\ 0.5407\,]\), and the power drops from \(\approx 0.47\) to \(1 - \Phi(0.680) + \Phi(-4.48) \approx 0.25\).

Demanding a smaller type I error rate (stricter “false alarm” control) moves the rejection threshold away from \(1/2\), making it harder to reject — the power falls by roughly a factor of two in both cases. This is the quantitative form of the type I / type II trade-off: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) cannot both be made arbitrarily small at a fixed \(n\); decreasing \(\alpha\) at fixed \(n\) increases \(\beta\) (decreases power).
